Interpretation Guide — reading the result

What to run, and how to read what comes back. This guide is about judgement, not syntax: which number to look at first, what a red flag means, and the two ways least squares will quietly mislead you. The equations are in the User Manual, Part I.

The order to read things

Read the report top-down and stop at the first failure — a break at one level makes the levels below it meaningless.

Two ways least squares lies to you

Lie 1 — “the residual is small, so the observation is good.” False. A residual shows only the fraction of the error present, . At even a huge blunder leaves a near-zero residual. Always pair a residual with its redundancy number. A small residual at low is the most dangerous reading in the whole report, not the most reassuring.
Lie 2 — “the tau test found no outliers, so the data are clean.” Pope’s tau normalises by , which the blunders themselves inflate. Past a few per-cent contamination the test goes blind — a 100σ error can pass. Read the contamination gauge before you trust any tau, and switch to a robust scale ('test','mad') when it is high.

The quadrant you must inspect

The bottom row is where reputations are lost. If a parameter you care about depends on bottom-row observations, no amount of clean residuals should reassure you; strengthen the geometry or add independent data.

Deciding what to do next

Level 1 failed — is it blunders or the model?

Run lsqvce.engine.systematic(D) before snooping. If the residuals show runs, serial correlation, or per-group offsets, the problem is the model (an unmodelled signal, a wrong weight ratio, coloured noise treated as white) — snooping it away just deletes good data around a defect that remains. Fix the model, then re-solve.

Diffuse contamination — reject or reweight?

Isolated blunders: iterative lsqvce.engine.snoop. Many small contaminations: robust M-estimation (lsqvce.engine.robust) degrades gracefully where snooping would chase its own tail. The contamination gauge tells you which regime you are in.

Group weights look wrong — variance components

If one data type or satellite is systematically over-fit or under-fit, run lsqvce.engine.varComp / lsqvce.engine.vce and read vc.sigma: a factor far from 1 means that group was mis-weighted going in. Re-solve at the converged weights before reading reliability — MDBs computed at wrong weights are wrong. Confirm vc.converged is true and that the minimum redundancy was met for every group. For in the thousands, the matrix-free Hutchinson backend (retired R5 M2) gives the same sigmas; see Choosing an engine.

Reading a gravity solution

For the block-arrow path (lsqvce.engine.adjustBlock / lsqvce.engine.adjustVCE), three habits keep you honest:

  • Diagnose through the accumulator bridge (lsqvce.engine.diagnoseAccum), never the reduced common system alone. The common-parameter redundancies are only correct once the arc-local parameters have been counted; skip the bridge and every reliability number is inflated.
  • Map the redundancy of the field, not just its residuals. A region carried by a single arc is a blind spot; its formal error can look fine while being uncheckable.
  • Watch for a near-singular arc. An arc with too few observations to fix its own state poisons the Schur complement; the accumulator flags it — do not force it in.
A clean bill of health means: Level 0 exact, Level 1 consistent, no high-w observations left, contamination low, and the parameters you care about backed by high-redundancy observations with acceptable MDB. Any one of those missing turns “passed” into “passed, but…”.
